Abstract

Interactive video world models let users explore generated environments, empowering applications from games to robotics. To achieve good quality, video world models often need to run on cloud GPUs, which can be costly for model deployers. Cloud deployment can also lead to higher response latency. A small on-device model can respond locally, but it loses details and drifts over long rollouts. Motivated by the complementary strengths of cloud and edge computing, we study how to deploy a video world model across heterogeneous edge devices and cloud GPUs. Heterogeneous deployment is challenging because current models typically require substantial cloud computation to maintain video quality, increasing serving cost and potentially delaying responses to user inputs. We present SPARE (Single-step Prediction and Action-conditioned Regeneration at the Edge), a simple collaboration method that combines one-step cloud prediction with on-device decoding and regeneration when the action changes. Compared with the original cloud-only generation, SPARE achieves 2.95× the throughput and reduces cloud GPU energy per second of generated video by a factor of 2.90, while maintaining similar visual quality.

Method

How SPARE splits the work

A chunk is a short group of video frames conditioned on the preceding video and the current action, such as a movement direction or no movement. Denoising steps predict a clean video latent from a noisy input. SPARE combines one-step cloud lookahead with local decoding and four-step regeneration when the action changes. Both pretrained models retain their weights.

One-step lookahead

While the current chunk is displayed, the cloud predicts the next chunk assuming the current action persists. It sends the x0 prediction from its first denoising step. The device’s VAE decoder converts this clean-latent estimate into frames, so the cloud does not run the decoder.

Device · 1.3B

Decoding and regeneration

If the action changes, the device discards the prediction made under the previous action. Its small model regenerates the chunk with all four denoising steps under the new action, without waiting for a cloud response. A device that cannot run the small model at the playback rate keeps only the decoder and leaves regeneration to the cloud (SPARE without the device generator).

Both

Shared history

Every displayed chunk enters both models’ generation histories, so the cloud continues from the regenerated chunk. More frequent action changes reduce the number of cloud predictions that can be reused. No cache tensor is transmitted between the models.

Results

Measured quality and cost

On 300 WorldScore scenes with actions changing every six seconds and no access to future actions, SPARE scores 7.07 out of 10 under a Qwen3-VL-32B evaluator, against 7.09 for four-step cloud-only generation. Throughput rises from 7.3 to 21.4 FPS (2.95×), and cloud GPU energy per second of video falls from 1,172 to 404 J (a factor of 2.90). These ratios use unrounded measured means. The total GPU energy of SPARE, 655 J, is higher than the 567 J of one-step cloud generation. Without the device generator, throughput is nearly unchanged (21.3 FPS) and VLM quality is 7.03, so local generation does not explain the throughput gain over four-step cloud generation. All results are for LingBot-World on workstation GPUs with injected network delay and 16-FPS playback.

Robustness

Playback and response under delay and outages

Local regeneration makes the first decoded frame available 0.20–0.41 s earlier than without the device generator, but the player’s buffer hides most of this gain in display latency. On WorldScore-300, a 2-s network outage reduces stalls from 1.33 to 0.29 s per clip, at 723 instead of 668 J total GPU energy per video second. These are injected-delay and outage tests on one host with a 16-FPS player, not a wide-area deployment, and outage video quality has not been scored.

Design space

Where to spend the cloud

We vary cloud sampling depth, device computation, and generation history. On LingBot-World, the one-step 14B model scores 7.03 against 7.09 for four steps and 6.49 for the four-step 1.3B model. On the 20-scene analysis set, the small model’s regenerated chunks are sharper with cloud-generated history than when it generates the rollout alone. We use one cloud step for LingBot-World and the small model for regeneration after action changes.

Second model pair

Beyond LingBot-World

We apply SPARE to Self-Forcing 1.3B on the device and Krea Realtime 14B in the cloud, both based on Wan2.1, with text prompts as actions that change four times per rollout and no advance access. The VLM quality score is 7.95 against 7.60 for one-step cloud (95% CI of the difference [0.26, 0.44]), although the reference knows future prompts; final-segment MUSIQ is 66.1 against 63.6, while whole-clip imaging quality stays below four-step cloud. Without the device generator, throughput falls from 9.8 to 8.8 FPS and cloud energy rises from 844 to 1,025 J. Four device steps take 0.48 s versus 0.94 s for one cloud step. Throughput remains below the playback rate, and this dynamic-prompt test has no network or playback clock.

Scope

Limitations

  • One-step chunks are softer than four-step chunks, so the imaging quality of SPARE stays below that of the cloud model (65.3 against 66.4 MUSIQ).
  • Device regeneration needs a device that runs the small model at the playback rate, here a workstation GPU (RTX PRO 6000). An RTX A5000 does not, and uses cloud regeneration with device decoding.
  • The device generator does not raise the judged quality in the reported comparison and does not explain the throughput gain. It saves 4% of cloud energy but costs 4% more total energy, and its benefits are earlier decoded-frame availability and fewer playback stalls during injected outages, subject to the playback buffer.
  • After an outage the device keeps generating until the cloud leads again, 16 of the 39 chunks of a clip on average, at the quality of the device model.
  • With an action change every 3 s the quality falls below the four-step cloud model (7.16 against 7.58), and the 120-s rollouts score 6.61 against 7.00. Action sequences differ across settings.
  • Actions are predefined piecewise-constant sequences, the visual scores come from automatic metrics and a VLM judge, and the cost is measured with both GPUs in one host, an injected network delay, and one cloud GPU per user. A human study, closed-loop play, and a wide-area deployment are future work.
